\subsection{JKP Factor Returns: A Structural Mismatch, Not a Failure}

JKP factor returns are market-level, time-series signals: they take the same value for all stocks within a given month, encoding information about recent factor-portfolio performance. This structure is well suited to time-series allocation or regime identification, but not to firm-level cross-sectional stock selection.

In a long-short portfolio, returns are measured as long minus short \textit{within} the same month. A prediction that is constant across stocks in a given month produces a random within-month ranking, yielding zero expected return before costs and negative net returns after. This explains why JKP-only yields undefined monthly Rank IC (within-month constant predictions) and $-4.5\%$ net annualised return --- not because JKP factors lack information, but because the information they carry is aligned with time-series variation rather than firm-level cross-sectional differences.

The \textit{global} Rank IC (Spearman across all stock-month pairs) can appear positive for JKP models because it captures cross-month variation: correctly calling ``good'' versus ``bad'' months inflates global IC even when within-month stock rankings are uninformative. The combination paradox (JKP + FinBERT has higher global IC than FinBERT alone yet worse portfolio performance) follows directly: added JKP features capture time-series variation that inflates global IC but degrades cross-sectional discrimination, while also raising portfolio turnover from 58\% to 91\% ($\approx$3\% additional annual transaction costs). This structural mismatch holds identically for XGBoost and deep learning models.

\subsection{XGBoost vs.\ Deep Learning}

\textbf{MLP captures mild non-linearities.} On the identical 12 FinBERT features, MLP\textsubscript{FinBERT} achieves IC 0.013 versus XGBoost's 0.009 --- both positive and both yielding profitable backtests. The Ridge linear benchmark (IC 0.002, Sharpe $-0.10$) confirms that the signal requires non-linear modelling, but the gap between MLP and XGBoost is modest. Deep learning adds value mainly by capturing pairwise sentiment feature interactions (e.g., high delta\_net combined with strong sentiment\_strength) that tree splits may partially miss.

\textbf{Sequence models do not generalise across regimes.} LSTM\textsubscript{FinBERT} and Attention\textsubscript{FinBERT} model the temporal trajectory of a firm's earnings calls over up to 8 quarters. Both models perform well in 2019--2020 but deteriorate sharply in 2021--2023 (see Appendix~\ref{app:rolling}). The quarterly trajectory patterns learned on 2008--2018 data do not transfer to the post-COVID rate-hike regime. Sequence models are less robust because quarterly earnings-call trajectories are sparse and regime-dependent; the cross-sectional rank of current sentiment is more regime-stable.

\textbf{Economic interpretation.} The results suggest that what matters is \textit{how a firm's tone ranks against peers today}, not \textit{how its tone has evolved over the past two years}. This is consistent with \cite{huang2023}, who show that one-quarter delta sentiment beats multi-quarter history.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Universe:} FinBERT models cover only earnings-call firms ($\approx$mid- and large-cap). Results may not generalise to small-cap or infrequently covered stocks.
    \item \textbf{Statistical power:} five years (60 months) of test data leave meaningful uncertainty around the Sharpe estimate; the IC $t$-test over 108 months ($t = 2.67$) provides stronger evidence of signal persistence.
    \item \textbf{Freshness cutoff:} the 3-month threshold was set a priori. Sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:fresh}) shows IC at 1-month cutoff is $3\times$ higher; the optimal cutoff for risk-adjusted backtest performance remains untested.
    \item \textbf{Transaction costs:} 0.2\% one-way is conservative for large-caps but may underestimate costs for smaller names. Sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:costs}) shows FinBERT-only remains profitable up to 0.4\% one-way.
    \item \textbf{JKP comparison:} JKP-only uses the full CRSP universe while FinBERT models use the freshness-filtered sub-universe; this partially confounds cross-model comparisons.
    \item \textbf{FinBERT context window:} the 512-token limit requires chunking; very long Q\&A sections may lose information in later chunks.
\end{enumerate}
